## Part 1 — About the practice

### Overview

Cerion Consulting's Data & Analytics practice helps organizations turn data into decisions. We start with the business question, build reliable data and analysis around it, and deliver something people can actually use: a dashboard, a report, a model or a clear recommendation.

Our work covers four areas:

1. **Business & Data Analytics:** Business Analytics, Statistical Analysis, KPI Development, Performance Reporting
2. **Business Intelligence & Data Engineering:** Tableau, Power BI, SQL, Database Management, Data Integration, Reporting Automation
3. **Machine Learning & Predictive Modeling:** Predictive Analytics, Forecasting, Classification, Clustering, Simulation
4. **Healthcare & Economic Consulting:** Healthcare Analytics, HEOR, Cost-Effectiveness Analysis, Economic Modeling, Healthcare Simulation

Data & Analytics is one of Cerion's practices, alongside AI Implementation, Salesforce and Blockchain.

### Our principles

- **Start with the decision, not the tool.** We first clarify what decision the work should support, who will use it, and what a useful result looks like.
- **Trustworthy numbers first.** Many reporting problems start upstream, in data definitions, joins and quality, so we fix the foundation before decorating it.
- **Fit for purpose.** We recommend the simplest solution that meets the need. We don't add infrastructure, models or complexity for their own sake.
- **Transparent methods.** Assumptions, definitions and limitations are written down and visible, not hidden inside a dashboard or a model.
- **Plain-language results.** Findings are explained so both technical and non-technical people can act on them.

### How we work

1. **Understand:** clarify the business question, the decision, the stakeholders and the available data.
2. **Analyze:** prepare, explore, validate and analyze the data with methods suited to the question.
3. **Translate:** turn findings into clear implications, trade-offs and recommendations.
4. **Deliver:** hand over a usable dashboard, report, model or recommendation, with documentation.

## Part 2 — The four practice areas

### 1. Business & Data Analytics

**Topics:** Business Analytics, Statistical Analysis, KPI Development, Performance Reporting

Teams often have more reports than clarity. We help define which questions matter, how performance should be measured, and what the data can and cannot answer.

**What we help with:**

- **Business analytics and exploratory data analysis (EDA):** understanding patterns, anomalies, relationships and the drivers of performance.
- **Statistical analysis:** testing whether differences and relationships are real, with appropriate confidence and sample sizes.
- **KPI development:** choosing KPIs that connect day-to-day activity to business outcomes, with clear definitions, owners and targets.
- **Performance reporting:** recurring reports and reviews that show how the business is performing against its KPIs.
- **Performance diagnostics:** working out why a number changed and whether it needs action.
- **Decision support:** framing findings so decision-makers can choose a practical next step.

**Questions this work answers:**

- Which KPIs best reflect our performance?
- Where are performance gaps occurring, and what is driving them?
- Which segments, products, channels or locations need attention?
- Did our initiative actually work, or would the result have happened anyway?
- How can our recurring reporting be made faster and more consistent?

### 2. Business Intelligence & Data Engineering

**Topics:** Tableau, Power BI, SQL, Database Management, Data Integration, Reporting Automation

A dashboard is only as good as the data behind it. We build both: the data foundations and the dashboards on top of them.

**What we help with:**

- **Tableau and Power BI dashboards:** executive, operational and exploratory dashboards designed around a specific decision or meeting.
- **Data visualization:** choosing the right charts, layout and drill-downs so changes and comparisons are easy to see.
- **SQL and database analytics:** querying, joining, transforming and validating data.
- **Database management and data modeling:** designing relational and conceptual data models (tables, keys, relationships) that reflect how the business actually works.
- **Data integration:** combining data from CRM, ERP, finance, e-commerce, spreadsheets and other systems into one consistent view.

- **Data quality checks:** automated checks that catch missing, duplicate, late or inconsistent data before it reaches a dashboard.
- **Reporting automation:** replacing manual, copy-paste reporting with repeatable, scheduled workflows.
- **Dashboard security:** row-level security so each user or client sees only the data they are allowed to see.
- **Dashboard and reporting audits:** reviewing existing dashboards, metrics and pipelines for errors, inconsistencies and improvements.

**Questions this work answers:**

- Why do two of our reports show different numbers?
- Where should our data live, and how should it be connected?
- Which checks should run before data reaches a dashboard?
- How fresh does our data really need to be?
- Which parts of our reporting can be automated?

### 3. Machine Learning & Predictive Modeling

**Topics:** Predictive Analytics, Forecasting, Classification, Clustering, Simulation

Predictive work is useful when it answers a planning question: what is likely to happen, what drives it, and what should we do about it?

**What we help with:**

- **Predictive analytics and regression:** estimating which factors drive an outcome and predicting future outcomes such as demand, churn or risk.
- **Forecasting and time-series analysis:** sales, demand, volume and cost forecasts that show uncertainty, not just a single number.
- **Classification:** models that sort cases into categories, such as likely to churn, high risk or needs urgent review, using methods such as logistic regression, decision trees, SVMs and neural networks.
- **Clustering and segmentation:** grouping similar customers, products or cases to reveal patterns.
- **Association analysis:** finding items or behaviors that commonly occur together.
- **Simulation and scenario analysis:** Monte Carlo simulation, scenario comparison and queueing models to test decisions before acting.
- **Model evaluation:** checking that a model generalizes, that its errors are acceptable for the business, and that it performs fairly across groups.
- **Decision thresholds:** turning model scores into clear action rules based on the real costs of different errors.

**Questions this work answers:**

- What is a realistic range of future outcomes?
- Which customers or cases share meaningful characteristics?
- How should false positives and false negatives be weighed?
- How much confidence should we place in the model?
- Which assumptions would change the plan?

### 4. Healthcare & Economic Consulting

**Topics:** Healthcare Analytics, HEOR, Cost-Effectiveness Analysis, Economic Modeling, Healthcare Simulation

Healthcare decisions involve outcomes, costs, timing and the population affected, all at once. We structure those questions so that definitions, comparators and assumptions are explicit.

**What we help with:**

- **Healthcare analytics:** utilization, outcomes, quality, patient flow and operational analysis.
- **Administrative healthcare data:** working with sources such as HCUP and with coding systems such as ICD-10-CM/PCS, CPT/HCPCS and DRGs.
- **HEOR (Health Economics and Outcomes Research):** measuring the clinical, economic and patient outcomes of treatments and programs.
- **Cost-effectiveness and cost-utility analysis:** comparing interventions by additional cost per additional health benefit (for example, cost per QALY).
- **Budget impact analysis:** estimating what adopting an intervention will cost a specific budget holder over the next few years.
- **Economic modeling:** decision trees, Markov models and microsimulation models.
- **Uncertainty analysis:** deterministic and probabilistic sensitivity analysis, scenario analysis and value-of-information analysis.
- **Healthcare simulation:** modeling patient flows, waiting times, capacity and scheduling.
- **Outcomes research methods:** risk adjustment, survival analysis, competing risks and avoiding common biases.

**Questions this work answers:**

- Is this intervention worth its extra cost, and can the budget afford it?
- How should costs and outcomes be defined for this question?
- Which assumptions drive the economic result?
- Are differences between hospitals or groups real, or due to patient mix?
- Where are the bottlenecks in our patient pathway?

### Business & data analytics

**What is business analytics?**
Business analytics is using data to understand how a business is performing and to make better decisions. It usually covers four questions: what happened (descriptive), why it happened (diagnostic), what is likely to happen (predictive), and what we should do (prescriptive).

**What is a KPI, and how is it different from a metric?**
A metric is any measured number. A KPI (key performance indicator) is one of the few metrics that directly reflects progress toward an important goal and is tied to a decision. Good KPIs have a clear definition, an owner, a target and an agreed action when they move.

**How do you help us choose the right KPIs?**
We start from your goals and the decisions you make, then build a KPI tree that connects strategy to measurable drivers. Each KPI gets an operational definition (exact formula, data source, filters, time period), an owner and a target. We document them in a KPI dictionary so everyone uses the same definitions.

**What are leading and lagging indicators?**
Lagging indicators measure results after the fact, such as revenue or churn. Leading indicators move earlier and help predict those results, such as pipeline volume or product usage. A good KPI set includes both.

**Which KPIs should go on a dashboard?**
Only the ones that support the decisions the dashboard is for. An executive dashboard usually needs a handful of outcome KPIs with targets and trends. An operational dashboard needs the drivers that teams can act on daily. Everything else belongs in drill-downs or separate reports.

**A KPI changed. How do we know if it's a real change or just noise?**
Every metric naturally fluctuates. We use methods such as control charts, which set expected limits based on normal variation, to separate real signals from noise. That way teams investigate meaningful changes instead of reacting to every movement.

**Our conversion rate went down even though every segment improved. How is that possible?**
This is usually caused by a change in mix, a form of Simpson's paradox. If more traffic comes from lower-converting segments, the overall average can fall even though each segment improves. We break the change down into "performance" and "mix" effects so you see what really happened.

**What is statistical analysis used for in business?**
To check whether differences and relationships in the data are real or due to chance. For example: did the new process really reduce delays, which factors are linked to sales, and how confident can we be in a survey result.

**Is our sample size large enough?**
It depends on how precise the answer needs to be for the decision. Sample size controls precision, not truth: a large sample can still be biased if it isn't representative. We also check that each subgroup you care about has enough data, since a sample can be large overall but tiny where it matters.

**What does "statistically significant" mean? Is it the same as important?**
Statistically significant means a result is unlikely to be due to chance alone. It does not mean the effect is big enough to matter for the business. We report both the size of the effect and the uncertainty around it.

**Can you help with A/B tests?**
Yes: test design, sample size planning, analysis and interpretation. We also check for common pitfalls, such as stopping a test as soon as it looks significant ("peeking"), which inflates false positives, and sample ratio mismatch, where the groups are not split as planned, which signals a technical problem.

**How can we measure the impact of a change if we didn't run an A/B test?**
Before-and-after comparisons are often misleading because other things change at the same time. Methods such as difference-in-differences compare the change in the affected group with the change in a similar comparison group, which gives a more credible estimate of impact.

**How should we handle missing data?**
It depends on why the data is missing. If the missing values are systematically different, for example unhappy customers not answering a survey, simply ignoring them or filling in averages can bias the result. We investigate the pattern and run sensitivity analyses to see whether missing data could change the decision.

**How do we know if our customer acquisition is actually profitable?**
Customer acquisition cost (CAC) alone doesn't tell you. We use cohort analysis to compare what each group of customers costs to acquire with the margin they generate over time, and calculate the payback period.

**What is cohort analysis?**
Grouping customers by a shared starting point, such as the month they joined or the channel they came from, and tracking their behavior over time. It shows trends in retention, revenue and profitability that overall averages hide.

**What is the difference between customer retention and net revenue retention?**
Customer retention measures the share of customers who stay. Net revenue retention (NRR) measures how much revenue from existing customers you keep, including upgrades, downgrades and churn. NRR can look strong even while many customers leave, if the remaining ones spend more, so both should be tracked.

**How should retention be measured when some customers joined recently?**
Recent customers haven't had time to churn yet, so a simple percentage can overstate retention. Survival analysis methods, such as Kaplan–Meier, account for customers who haven't been observed for the full period.

**Can you help with pricing decisions?**
Yes. We analyze price elasticity (how demand changes when price changes) and model profit, not just revenue, at different price points. Where historical data isn't enough, we can help design a pricing test.

**Our business is profitable, so why are we short on cash?**
Profit and cash are different. Cash can be tied up in unpaid customer invoices (receivables) and inventory. Working capital analytics shows where cash is tied up and how changes such as faster collections would affect it.

### Business intelligence & dashboards

**Do you build dashboards?**
Yes. We build interactive dashboards in Tableau and Power BI for executives, operations and analysis. They are designed around the decisions they support and update automatically.

**Tableau or Power BI: which should we use?**
Both are excellent. Power BI often fits organizations already using Microsoft 365 and Azure and is usually more cost-effective at scale. Tableau is known for flexible, highly visual exploration. The best choice depends on your existing tools, budget, users and data sources, and we can help you decide.

**Can you improve or fix our existing dashboards?**
Yes. We review measure definitions, data logic, performance, chart choices and usability, and fix what is wrong or confusing.

**What makes a good dashboard?**
It answers specific questions for a specific audience, shows a few key measures with context (targets, trends, comparisons), uses charts that make change easy to see, and lets users drill down without being overwhelmed. Its definitions are documented, and its data is checked.

**How do you choose the right chart?**
By the question being asked. Use line charts for change over time, bar charts for comparing categories, waterfall charts for what contributed to a change, maps for location, and Sankey charts for flows. Color is used to carry information, not as decoration.

**Why do two of our reports show different numbers?**
Common causes:
- different definitions of the same metric (for example, revenue before or after refunds)
- different date logic (order date versus payment date)
- different filters or time zones
- duplicated rows from joins
- data that arrived late
- different data refresh times

We trace each number back to its source and agree on one definition.

**Why is our dashboard double-counting?**
Usually because of data grain: tables at different levels of detail are joined, for example one row per order joined to one row per order item, which repeats values. The fix is to define what one row represents, aggregate before joining, and add checks that catch row-count explosions.

**What is data grain?**
Grain is what one row in a table represents, for example one order, one customer per month, or one patient visit. Mixing grains is one of the most common causes of wrong dashboard numbers.

**Why did last week's numbers change?**
Often because some data arrived late, such as delayed transactions, refunds or system syncs, so past periods get updated. We can design reporting that marks recent periods as preliminary, settles them after a cutoff, or freezes historical snapshots.

**Why did historical sales move between territories or segments?**
If the CRM overwrites a customer's territory or segment, past sales get reassigned to the new value. Keeping the history of these changes (a "slowly changing dimension") lets reports show what was true at the time of each sale.

**How often should our dashboards refresh?**
As often as the decision needs, not as often as technology allows. A monthly strategy report may be fine with daily data, while inventory or operations dashboards may need hourly updates. More frequent refreshes cost more, so we match refresh frequency to the value of fresher data.

**Can different clients or users see only their own data?**
Yes, with row-level security (RLS). Security rules applied in the database or in Power BI/Tableau control which rows each user can see. Dashboard filters alone are not security.

**Can you automate our recurring reports?**
Yes. We replace manual exports and spreadsheet work with automated pipelines and scheduled reports, which saves time and reduces errors.

### Data engineering & databases

**Can you combine data from multiple sources?**
Yes. We integrate data from CRMs, ERPs, accounting, e-commerce, marketing platforms, databases and spreadsheets into one consistent view.

**Can you clean messy data?**
Yes. Data preparation is a core part of most projects: standardizing formats, removing duplicates, fixing invalid values, and making these steps repeatable so they don't have to be redone by hand.

**Do we need a data warehouse?**
Not always. Signs that you might:
- you repeatedly combine data from several systems
- you need reliable history
- your queries are getting complex
- teams report different numbers for the same metric
- critical reporting depends on one person's spreadsheets

If your needs are simple, a lighter setup may be enough, and we can help you assess this.

**What is a data warehouse?**
A central analytical database where data from different business systems is collected, standardized, integrated, kept with history and modeled for reporting. It keeps analysis separate from the systems that run day-to-day operations.

**What is ETL or ELT?**
Extract, Transform, Load (or Extract, Load, Transform): the process of moving data from source systems, cleaning and reshaping it, and loading it into an analytical database. ELT loads the raw data first and transforms it inside the warehouse.

**Should we use real-time data (CDC) or nightly batch loads?**
Choose the simplest option that meets the business need. Nightly or hourly batch loads are enough for most reporting. Change Data Capture (CDC), which streams database changes continuously, is worth its extra complexity when decisions really depend on very recent data, or when updates and deletions must be tracked accurately.

**What are data quality checks?**
Automated tests that run before data is used, for example:
- completeness (did all the data load?)
- uniqueness (are there duplicates?)
- validity (are values in the expected range?)
- freshness (is the data up to date?)
- reconciliation (do totals match the source system?)

They stop bad data from silently reaching dashboards.

**A system changed its fields and our dashboards broke. How do we prevent that?**
With data contracts: agreed definitions of what data a source provides (fields, types, units, meaning), plus checks that alert you when a change breaks them. This catches problems such as a field switching from dollars to cents before they distort reports.

**Our CRM and billing system show different customer counts. Which is right?**
Possibly both: they may count different things, such as contacts versus accounts. Customer identity resolution defines what a "customer" means and links records across systems with a reliable mapping, so you can report one consistent number.

**Can you design or improve our database?**
Yes. We design relational and conceptual data models (entities, relationships, keys) based on your business rules, so data is consistent and easy to query.

**Our SQL queries or database are slow and expensive. Can you help?**
Yes. We audit query performance by measuring the baseline, reading execution plans, and finding unnecessary scans, missing indexes or partitions, and inefficient joins. Often this reduces both run time and cloud costs before you need to add more capacity.

### Machine learning & predictive modeling

**What is predictive analytics?**
Using historical data and statistical or machine learning models to estimate future outcomes, such as which customers may churn, how much demand to expect, or which cases are high risk.

**What is the difference between predictive analytics and machine learning?**
Predictive analytics is the goal: predicting outcomes. Machine learning is one family of methods for doing it, alongside classical statistical models such as regression. We choose the simplest method that answers the question reliably.

**Do we have enough data for machine learning?**
It depends on the problem, the number of outcomes you want to predict, and data quality. We assess this early. Sometimes a simpler statistical model, or improving data collection first, is the better choice.

**Can you forecast our sales or demand?**
Yes. We build forecasts using time-series and statistical methods that show a realistic range, not just one number. We also evaluate forecasts over time.

**How do you measure forecast accuracy?**
With measures suited to your data and decisions:
- MAE: average error in units
- MAPE: percentage error, which breaks down with zero or very small values
- WAPE: total error relative to total actual demand
- MASE: compared with a simple baseline forecast

We also check bias, meaning whether the forecast is consistently too high or too low.

**Our forecast is accurate, so why do we still run out of stock?**
Because forecasting demand and deciding how much inventory to hold are different problems. Inventory decisions also need to account for forecast uncertainty, supplier lead times, safety stock and service-level targets. We connect forecasts to reorder points and stock policies.

**Our regional forecasts don't add up to the national forecast. How do we fix that?**
With hierarchical forecast reconciliation, which adjusts forecasts at all levels (product, region, total) so they add up consistently while staying as accurate as possible.

**What is classification?**
A type of model that assigns cases to categories, for example likely to churn or not, or urgent or not urgent. Common methods include logistic regression, decision trees, random forests, support vector machines and neural networks.

**What is clustering?**
A method that groups similar items without predefined labels, for example customer segments based on behavior. It is often used for segmentation and targeting.

**Our model is 90% accurate. Is that good?**
Not necessarily. Accuracy can hide important failures, especially when one outcome is rare. We look at precision, recall and the business cost of each type of error, and check performance for important subgroups.

**What is a classification threshold, and why does it matter?**
A model gives a score or probability; the threshold decides at what score you take action. The default 0.5 is rarely the right choice. The best threshold depends on the cost of false positives versus false negatives and on your team's capacity to act.

**What is the difference between AUC and calibration?**
AUC measures how well a model ranks cases from higher to lower risk. Calibration measures whether its probabilities are correct, for example whether cases scored 30% actually happen about 30% of the time. If you use probabilities to make cost-based decisions, calibration matters too.

**Why do models perform well in testing but fail after launch?**
A common cause is data leakage: the model was trained using information that won't be available at prediction time, or test data overlapped with training data. We use time-based and grouped validation to get honest performance estimates.

**When should a model be retrained?**
When monitoring shows its performance or input data has changed meaningfully ("drift"), not just on a fixed calendar. We set up monitoring so you know when retraining is needed.

**How do you make sure a model is fair?**
We check performance, such as error rates and recall, across relevant groups, not just overall. A model can look good on average while failing for a specific group.

**Should we target the customers most likely to churn?**
Not always. Some high-risk customers will leave no matter what, and some low-risk customers would stay anyway. Uplift modeling estimates who will actually change their behavior because of an offer, which often makes retention budgets go further.

**What is simulation, and when is it useful?**
Simulation models a process or decision many times under different assumptions to see the range of possible outcomes. It is useful when outcomes depend on uncertainty, timing or interacting factors, such as capacity planning, risk analysis or budget planning.

**Scenario analysis or Monte Carlo simulation: what's the difference?**
Scenario analysis compares a few defined cases, such as best, base and worst. Monte Carlo simulation runs thousands of random combinations of uncertain inputs to show the full range of outcomes and their probabilities, for example the probability of a loss.

**Is it worth collecting more data before deciding?**
Value-of-information analysis estimates how much better your decision could become with more information, and whether that is worth the cost of a study, survey or pilot.

**How many staff do we need to keep waiting times acceptable?**
Queueing models such as Erlang C estimate waiting times for different staffing levels in support centers, clinics and similar services. Planning only for average workload usually underestimates staffing needs, especially at peak times.

**Where should we open a new location, and will it take sales from our existing ones?**
Location models such as the Huff model estimate how customers choose between locations and how much of a new site's revenue would come from your existing sites (cannibalization) rather than new customers.

### Healthcare & economic consulting

**Do you work in healthcare?**
Yes. Our Healthcare & Economic Consulting area covers healthcare analytics, HEOR, cost-effectiveness analysis, economic modeling and healthcare simulation.

**What is HEOR?**
HEOR stands for Health Economics and Outcomes Research. It measures the clinical, economic and patient outcomes of treatments, technologies and programs to support decisions by health systems, payers, providers and manufacturers.

**What is cost-effectiveness analysis?**
A method that compares two or more healthcare options by their additional cost and additional health benefit. The result is often expressed as an ICER: the extra cost per extra unit of health gained.

**What is an ICER?**
The incremental cost-effectiveness ratio: the difference in cost between two options divided by the difference in health outcomes, often cost per QALY gained. It is compared with a willingness-to-pay threshold to judge value for money.

**What is a QALY?**
A quality-adjusted life year combines length and quality of life into one measure. One year in perfect health equals 1 QALY; a year in a health state valued at 0.7 equals 0.7 QALYs.

**What is the difference between cost-effectiveness and cost-utility analysis?**
Cost-utility analysis is a type of cost-effectiveness analysis that measures health benefit in QALYs, which makes different diseases and interventions comparable.

**What is net monetary benefit?**
Health gain converted into money using a willingness-to-pay threshold, minus the additional cost. A positive net monetary benefit means the option is cost-effective at that threshold. It is often easier to interpret than an ICER.

**Cost-effective means cheaper, right?**
No. Cost-effective means the extra health benefit is worth the extra cost. An intervention can be good value per patient and still be expensive for the total budget.

**What is budget impact analysis, and how is it different from cost-effectiveness?**
Cost-effectiveness asks whether an intervention is good value. Budget impact analysis asks whether a specific budget holder can afford it over the next few years, given the eligible population, how fast it is adopted, and cost offsets. Decision-makers usually need both.

**How does patient uptake affect budget impact?**
Strongly. The same intervention costs very different amounts per year depending on how quickly patients start using it. We model uptake curves and scenarios such as fast versus phased adoption.

**Decision tree or Markov model: which should be used?**
Decision trees suit short, well-defined sequences of events, such as a one-time test or procedure. Markov models suit conditions where patients move between health states over time, such as chronic diseases. The right model is the simplest one that captures the differences that matter.

**What is microsimulation, and when is it better than a Markov model?**
Microsimulation simulates individual patients one at a time. It is worth the extra complexity when patient characteristics, disease history, treatment switching or competing events strongly affect outcomes, which simpler cohort models can't capture well.

**What is sensitivity analysis?**
Testing how results change when assumptions change. Deterministic sensitivity analysis varies one or a few inputs at a time, such as in a tornado diagram. Probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA) varies all uncertain inputs at once to show how confident we can be in the conclusion.

**What is a cost-effectiveness acceptability curve?**
A chart, produced from probabilistic sensitivity analysis, that shows the probability each option is cost-effective across a range of willingness-to-pay thresholds.

**What healthcare data do you work with?**
Administrative and claims data, hospital discharge and emergency department data (such as HCUP datasets), and coded clinical data using ICD-10-CM/PCS, CPT/HCPCS and DRGs. `TODO: confirm data sources`

**What is HCUP?**
The Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project, a family of US hospital databases from AHRQ (the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality). It includes inpatient, emergency department and ambulatory surgery data and is widely used for utilization, cost and outcomes research.

**What do ICD-10, CPT/HCPCS and DRG codes mean?**
- **ICD-10-CM:** codes diagnoses.
- **ICD-10-PCS:** codes inpatient procedures.
- **CPT/HCPCS:** code medical services and procedures, mainly outpatient.
- **DRGs:** group hospital stays for payment purposes.

Correctly defining populations and outcomes with these codes is essential for reliable analysis.

**Are hospital charges the same as costs?**
No. Charges are list prices, costs are the resources used, and payments are what is actually paid. To estimate costs from charges, analysts commonly apply cost-to-charge ratios (CCRs). Comparing hospitals on charges alone can be misleading.

**Why do raw readmission rates make some hospitals look worse than they are?**
Hospitals treat patients with different levels of risk. Risk adjustment and standardization account for patient mix so comparisons are fair. Without them, a hospital treating sicker patients can look worse even if it performs better within every risk group.

**What are competing risks, and why do they matter?**
A competing risk is an event that prevents the outcome of interest. For example, death prevents a later readmission. Ignoring this can overestimate risk, so methods such as cumulative incidence functions are used instead.

**What is immortal time bias?**
A common error in treatment studies: the time before a patient starts treatment is wrongly counted as "treated" time, which makes the treatment look more beneficial than it is. Defining the start of follow-up correctly, for example with target trial emulation, avoids it.

**Can you help reduce hospital or clinic waiting times?**
Yes. We analyze patient flow and capacity using queueing theory and simulation. Waiting times rise sharply as capacity approaches full use, so small capacity changes can have large effects.

**Can you help with clinic scheduling and no-shows?**
Yes. We model attendance and overbooking policies to balance patient access, overtime and waiting times, rather than simply overbooking by the average no-show rate.

**Can you analyze gaps in patient follow-up?**
Yes. We measure each step of a care pathway, such as a test result followed by review, communication and follow-up visit, to find where patients drop out and which improvements would help most.
